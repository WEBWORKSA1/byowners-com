This guide is for owners who want the reach of the multiple listing service without paying a full listing commission. You'll learn what a flat-fee MLS listing is, what it typically costs, how buyer-agent pay works under the 2024 rules, and how to pick a provider without getting caught by fine print.

## What a flat-fee MLS listing is

A **multiple listing service (MLS)** is a database of homes for sale, run by local broker groups, that agents use to find homes for their buyers. Only licensed brokers who are MLS members can enter listings, which is why a pure FSBO seller can't post there directly. Many public home-search websites pull their listings from MLS feeds, so an MLS listing also tends to show up widely online.

A **flat-fee MLS** broker enters your home in the MLS for a fixed fee instead of a percentage commission. You sign a listing agreement, the broker becomes the listing broker of record, and you handle almost everything else: pricing, showings, offers, and negotiation. Some providers sell add-ons that fill in more of the gaps.

The service sits between two choices. NAR's 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers found that 5% of sellers sold without an agent, while 91% used one. Flat-fee MLS gives you the exposure of the 91% and the cost structure closer to the 5%.

## What it costs and what's included

Published flat-fee packages commonly start around $100 for a bare MLS entry and run to $1,000 or more for bundles with photography, a lockbox, and help with contracts. Price depends on your state, your MLS's fees, and the provider. The tiers usually look like this:

| Package level | Usually includes | Common extras or limits |
|---|---|---|
| Basic | MLS entry for a fixed term, a capped number of photos, syndication to public sites | Fees to change price or photos; short term; no sign or lockbox |
| Mid-tier | More photos, unlimited changes, yard sign, lockbox, showing scheduling | Professional photos often extra |
| Full-featured | Professional photos, open house listings, offer review, contract preparation or attorney referral | Closing fee or percentage due at sale in some plans |

### Fees to look for in the fine print

- **Closing fees.** Some plans add a fee or small percentage payable when the home sells. Know the total, not just the upfront price.
- **Cancellation fees.** Ask what it costs to withdraw if you sell on your own or change your mind.
- **Change fees.** A price cut you can't make without paying slows you down when timing matters.
- **Term.** Ask how many months the listing lasts and what renewal costs.

A handful of states have "minimum service" laws that require listing brokers to provide certain services, such as receiving and presenting offers. In those states, flat-fee packages may include more, and cost more, by law.

## How buyer-agent pay works after the 2024 changes

Two practice changes took effect on August 17, 2024, as part of the settlement of commission lawsuits involving the National Association of Realtors:

1. **Offers of compensation to buyers' agents are prohibited in the MLS.** The listing can't include a field saying what you'll pay a buyer's broker.
2. **Agents working with buyers must have a written agreement with the buyer before touring a home.** That agreement sets what the buyer's agent will be paid and by whom.

What this means for you as a flat-fee seller:

- **You can still offer to pay a buyer's agent.** You just can't advertise it in the MLS. It can be communicated off-MLS or simply negotiated when an offer arrives.
- **Expect the request in the offer.** A buyer whose agreement says their agent earns 2.5% may ask you to pay all or part of it. That's a negotiable term, like the price.
- **Concessions are a separate tool.** You can offer a closing-cost credit to the buyer instead. Rules on advertising concessions vary by MLS, so ask your broker what's allowed.
- **Put it in the contract.** Any fee you agree to pay should be written into the purchase agreement and show up on the settlement statement.

For the full background, see [real estate commission rule changes explained](/guides/real-estate-commission-rule-changes-explained/).

### The math

Example: on a $450,000 sale, a 5.5% total commission is $24,750. With a $500 flat-fee package and a buyer's agent paid 2.5% ($11,250), your cost is $11,750, saving $13,000. If the buyer is unrepresented, your cost is the $500 package plus your legal fees. These figures are illustrative; every commission is negotiable.

## Pros and cons

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| MLS exposure to agents and their buyers | You still do the agent's work: pricing, showings, negotiation |
| Wide syndication to public home-search sites | Buyers' agents may contact you constantly and may assume you'll pay their fee |
| Predictable, low upfront cost | Add-on and closing fees can creep up |
| You keep control of price and showings | Limited or no advice from the broker in basic plans |
| Can be combined with by-owner marketing | You carry more legal risk without attorney help on the contract |

## How to choose a flat-fee broker

Use these questions before you pay anyone:

1. Is the broker licensed in your state and a member of the MLS that covers your neighborhood? Check your state real estate commission's license lookup.
2. What exactly is included, for how many months, and how many photos?
3. Can you change the price, photos, and description at no charge, and how fast do changes go live?
4. Is any fee or percentage due at closing?
5. What does it cost to cancel, and do you owe anything if you sell to a buyer you find yourself?
6. How are buyer-agent calls, showing requests, and offers routed to you, and how quickly?
7. Is a lockbox or showing service included?
8. Will the broker help review offers or prepare the contract, or should you hire an attorney?
9. How will the broker communicate any buyer-agent compensation you choose to offer, given the MLS restriction?

## What daily life looks like with a flat-fee listing

Within a day or two of going live, your listing typically appears in the MLS and on the public search sites that pull from it. Then the phone starts ringing, and most early calls come from agents, not buyers.

- **Compensation questions.** Buyers' agents will ask what you're offering their brokerage. Decide your answer before launch: a set amount, "negotiable with an offer," or a closing-cost credit instead. Give the same answer every time.
- **Showing requests.** If your package includes a showing service, requests arrive by text or app; otherwise agents contact you directly. Reply within an hour or two during the first weeks, because buyers book several homes in one trip.
- **Feedback.** Ask each showing agent one question afterward: what would keep your buyer from making an offer? Patterns in those answers tell you whether price, condition, or photos are the problem.
- **Offers.** Agents usually send offers on their state or local association's standard forms, with a pre-approval letter and an earnest money amount. Read every deadline, and have your attorney review the offer before you sign or counter.

Your broker of record may need copies of the signed contract and closing documents to update the listing's MLS status, so keep them informed when you go under contract and when you close.

## Is flat-fee MLS right for you?

**Good fit:** your area is busy enough that most buyers work with agents, you're comfortable pricing from comps, and you have time for showings. A conventional house in a popular neighborhood is the classic case.

**Probably not:** you already have a buyer (a pure FSBO sale with an attorney costs less), or the sale is complicated by probate, a short sale, or tenants, where a full-service agent or attorney earns their fee.

**In between:** if you want MLS exposure but dread negotiating, pair a flat-fee listing with an attorney who reviews offers by the hour. Our guide to [negotiating offers without an agent](/guides/negotiating-offers-without-an-agent/) covers the rest.
