This guide is for homeowners who are selling by owner and want their home to look its best online and in person without paying for a full staging company. When you finish it, you'll have a room-by-room plan, a budget at three spending levels, the exact phone settings and shot list for your listing photos, and a clear rule for using virtual staging honestly.

## Start with the edit: declutter and depersonalize

Buyers scroll past listings in seconds, and clutter is the first thing a camera exaggerates. A good working rule: remove about a third of what's in every room, then look again.

- **Surfaces:** Clear kitchen counters down to two or three items. Bathroom counters get nothing but a soap dispenser and a folded towel.
- **Closets and storage:** Pack out half of what's in them. Buyers open closet doors, and half-empty reads as "plenty of space."
- **Furniture:** Remove pieces that block walkways or make rooms look small. Extra dining chairs and oversized recliners are common culprits.
- **Personal items:** Pack family photos, kids' artwork, mail, calendars, and anything with names or schedules. This helps buyers picture themselves there, and it keeps your family's details off the internet.

Then **deep clean**: windows inside and out, baseboards, light fixtures, grout, and the inside of the oven and fridge. Deal with odors at the source, especially pets, smoke, and drains. A clean home photographs brighter, and buyers notice smell before anything else at a showing.

Store what you've packed off-site or stack it neatly in the garage, and photograph the garage last.

## Room-by-room staging

### Living room

Pull furniture off the walls into a conversation grouping, even a few inches. Use one rug to anchor the seating. Add two or three pillows and a throw in a single accent color, and leave the coffee table nearly empty: a tray, a plant, or a stack of two books.

### Kitchen

Remove small appliances except perhaps a coffee maker. Replace dated cabinet hardware if it's an easy swap, and add one bowl of fruit or a plant for color. Make sure every bulb works and matches in color.

### Primary bedroom

Make the bed the focal point: white or neutral bedding, two sets of pillows, and a folded throw. Matching nightstands and lamps make the room look intentional. Clear the tops of dressers.

### Bathrooms

New white towels, a fresh shower curtain or a gleaming glass door, and a closed toilet lid in every photo. Re-caulk the tub if the old caulk is stained; it's a cheap fix that photographs as "well maintained."

### Secondary bedrooms and flex spaces

Give every room one clear purpose. A spare room crammed with exercise gear and boxes should become a guest room or a simple office with a desk and chair.

### Outdoor living

Sweep the patio, power wash the deck, and set out a small table with two chairs. Buyers respond to a usable outdoor room more than to a large empty yard.

## Curb appeal: your first photo

The front exterior is usually the first photo a buyer sees, so it decides whether they click.

- Mow, edge, and add fresh mulch to beds.
- Trim shrubs below window sills so the windows show.
- Power wash the walkway, driveway, and siding.
- Paint or refinish the front door and replace dated house numbers and the light fixture.
- Add a new doormat and two potted plants flanking the entry.
- Remove cars, trash bins, hoses, and toys before every photo and showing.

## What to spend: $0, $500, and $2,000 staging budgets

These tiers are illustrative; local prices vary.

| Budget | What it covers | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| $0 | Declutter, deep clean, rearrange furniture, borrow decor from other rooms, open blinds, trim the yard yourself | Occupied homes in good condition |
| About $500 | Everything above, plus mulch and plants, new towels and bedding, matching warm-white bulbs, a few pillows and throws, cabinet hardware, touch-up paint | Most occupied homes; the best return for the money |
| About $2,000 | Everything above, plus repainting the main living areas a neutral color, a few rented or secondhand furniture pieces for empty rooms, a professional deep clean, and professional photos | Vacant homes or homes with dated paint and heavy wear |

If your home was built before 1978, be careful with any painting prep that involves sanding or scraping. Old paint may contain lead, and the EPA recommends lead-safe work practices. Contractors you pay for that kind of work generally must be certified under the EPA's lead renovation rules.

## Smartphone photography settings that look professional

A recent smartphone can take listing photos that hold their own. The difference between amateur and professional usually comes down to technique.

- **Use a tripod** and the self-timer or a remote, so every shot is sharp and level.
- **Shoot in landscape orientation.** Listing sites display horizontal photos best.
- **Turn on the grid** and keep vertical lines in walls and door frames straight. Hold the phone level rather than tilting it up or down.
- **Shoot at about chest height,** not eye level, so rooms don't look like you're peering down at them.
- **Use the main 1x lens where you can.** The ultrawide lens helps in small rooms, but stand in a corner and keep furniture away from the frame edges, where distortion stretches it.
- **Leave HDR on or on auto** to balance bright windows and darker corners.
- **Tap to focus and lock exposure** on a mid-tone wall, then nudge brightness up slightly if the room looks dim.
- **Turn the flash off** and avoid digital zoom.
- **Turn on every light** and use bulbs of the same color temperature so walls don't look half yellow, half blue.
- **Edit lightly:** straighten, correct white balance, and brighten. Don't oversaturate the lawn or the sky.

## A shot list in the right order

Lead with your strongest images. A typical single-family listing works well in this order:

1. Front exterior, straight on or at a slight angle
2. Front exterior from a second angle showing the lot
3. Entry or foyer looking in
4. Living room from the corner that shows the most space
5. Living room reverse angle
6. Kitchen wide shot
7. Kitchen detail: island, range, or pantry
8. Dining area
9. Family room or den
10. Primary bedroom
11. Primary bathroom
12. Secondary bedrooms, one photo each
13. Other bathrooms
14. Office, laundry, and bonus spaces
15. Backyard, patio, and deck
16. Any view, garage, or standout feature last

Skip photos of nearly identical angles and rooms that add nothing, such as a bare utility closet.

## Timing and light

**Exteriors:** Shoot the front when sunlight falls on the facade, not when the sun is behind the house throwing it into shadow. As a rule, an east-facing front looks best in the morning and a west-facing front in the afternoon. A bright overcast day avoids harsh shadows entirely. If your home has great exterior lighting, a dusk photo taken shortly after sunset, with every interior light on, can make a striking lead image.

**Interiors:** Shoot mid-morning or mid-afternoon with all blinds open and lights on. Avoid direct sun beams across floors and furniture, which blow out to pure white. Overcast days are an interior photographer's friend.

## Virtual staging: helpful, if you're honest about it

Virtual staging adds digital furniture to photos of empty rooms. It's inexpensive and helps buyers understand scale and layout. It's also easy to misuse. Follow these rules:

- **Label every edited photo** as virtually staged, right in the caption or image.
- **Include an unedited photo** of each virtually staged room.
- **Add furniture and decor only.** Never remove or alter permanent features: cracks, stains, power lines, a neighboring building, the view, flooring, or fixtures.
- **Keep it realistic.** Furniture should fit the actual room size, and the style should match the home.

Real estate agents who are NAR members are bound by a Code of Ethics that requires them to present a true picture in their advertising, and that's a useful standard for owners too. Photos that hide defects can expose you to misrepresentation claims under state law, and they backfire anyway when buyers walk in and feel misled.

One more reason to keep your original files: the FTC warns that rental scammers copy photos from real listings to advertise homes they don't control. After you list, search your address now and then to make sure your photos aren't being used in a fake rental ad.
