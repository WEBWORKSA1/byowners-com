This guide is for owners who sold without an agent and now have a signed contract to take to the finish line. You'll learn who runs the closing, how title work and title insurance protect the deal, what happens week by week, how to protect your proceeds from wire fraud, and what to expect at the closing table and after.

## Who runs your closing

Every sale needs a neutral party to hold the money, clear title, prepare the settlement statement, and record the deed. Depending on your state and local custom, that's a title company, an escrow company, a real estate attorney, or some combination.

| | Title or escrow company | Real estate attorney |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Neutral party following the contract and written instructions | Represents a client and, in some states, also acts as closing agent |
| Typical tasks | Title search, title insurance, holding funds, settlement statement, recording | Contract drafting or review, title review, deed preparation, running the closing |
| Gives legal advice | No | Yes, to their client |
| When it's used | The norm in many states | Required or customary in some states |

In some states a real estate attorney must conduct the closing or prepare the deed; in others, attorneys are optional. Practice can differ even by county. Rules vary by state, so confirm with a local real estate attorney before you choose.

Choose your closing agent as soon as you accept an offer, and send them the fully signed contract. If the buyer is financing, the lender will coordinate with whoever you choose. Note that a seller can't require the buyer to purchase title insurance from a particular title company as a condition of the sale; federal settlement law prohibits it.

## Title search and title insurance

The **title search** reviews public records for the property's chain of ownership and anything attached to it: mortgages, home equity lines, tax liens, judgments, mechanics' liens, easements, and HOA liens. The result is a **title commitment** that lists what must happen before the title company will insure the sale, such as paying off your mortgage, recording a release for an old loan, or providing a trust certificate or death certificate.

**Title insurance** comes in two forms:

- **Lender's policy:** protects the buyer's lender and is almost always required for a financed purchase.
- **Owner's policy:** protects the buyer's ownership against covered title defects, such as a forged deed in the chain or an undisclosed heir.

Who pays for the owner's policy is set by local custom and your contract. In some areas the seller pays; in others the buyer does. It's negotiable either way.

## The closing timeline

Here's an example timeline for a financed sale. Cash sales skip the appraisal and underwriting and can close faster once title is clear.

| Timing (example) | What happens | Who handles it | Your job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day 0 | Contract fully signed | You and the buyer | Send the signed contract to the closing agent |
| Days 1–3 | Escrow opened; earnest money deposited | Closing agent | Confirm the deposit was received |
| Days 1–10 | Inspection period | Buyer and inspector | Provide access; respond to repair requests in writing |
| Days 3–14 | Title search and commitment; payoff ordered | Title company or attorney | Sign payoff authorization; answer title questions |
| Days 7–21 | Appraisal | Lender and appraiser | Provide access and a list of upgrades with dates |
| Days 14–30 | Underwriting and loan approval | Lender | Finish agreed repairs; deliver HOA documents |
| At least 3 business days before closing | Buyer receives the Closing Disclosure | Lender | Review your draft settlement statement |
| 1–2 days before closing | Final walk-through | Buyer | House clean and empty; repairs done; keys ready |
| Closing day | Signing, funding, disbursement | Closing agent | Sign the deed and seller documents; bring ID |
| Closing day to a few days after | Deed recorded; loan paid off; proceeds sent | Closing agent and county recorder | Confirm payoff and watch for your lien release |

The three-business-day rule for the buyer's Closing Disclosure is federal law. If certain loan terms change late, the clock can restart, so avoid last-minute changes to the deal.

## Your pre-closing checklist

1. Send the signed contract and all addenda to the closing agent.
2. Give the closing agent your loan account numbers and sign a payoff authorization for every mortgage and home equity line.
3. Answer title questions quickly, and provide trust, divorce, or estate documents if title requires them.
4. Order HOA resale documents if you haven't already.
5. Complete agreed repairs with licensed contractors and keep receipts.
6. Schedule final utility readings for closing day, but keep service on through the walk-through.
7. Ask what identification and documents to bring, and whether you can sign in advance.
8. Confirm how you'll receive your proceeds, in person or by a verified phone call.
9. Gather keys, garage remotes, alarm codes, and appliance manuals.
10. Move out and leave the house in the condition your contract requires.

## Wire fraud: protect your proceeds

Closing funds attract criminals. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center recorded 12,368 real estate fraud complaints and $275.1 million in reported losses in 2025, a category that includes wire fraud tied to closings. The typical scheme is an email that looks like it came from the title company, attorney, or lender, with "updated" wiring instructions.

The CFPB's advice applies to sellers as much as buyers:

- Confirm wiring instructions, including account name and number, in person or by calling a phone number you already have, never one in an email.
- Treat any change to payment instructions as a red flag; closing agents rarely change them at the last minute.
- Avoid sending account details by unencrypted email.
- After the money moves, call to confirm it arrived.

For your proceeds, give your bank details to the closing agent in person or by a verified call, and ask how they verify disbursement instructions. If anything feels off, stop and call. A one-day delay is cheap; a misdirected wire is often unrecoverable.

## What happens at the closing table

In some areas everyone signs together; in others, buyer and seller sign separately, sometimes days apart. As the seller you'll typically sign:

- **The deed** transferring title to the buyer
- **The settlement statement** showing the price, your payoffs, prorated property taxes, fees, any credits, and your net proceeds
- **An owner's or title affidavit** confirming no unrecorded liens, unpaid contractors, or other occupants
- **A certificate of non-foreign status** under FIRPTA. If you're a foreign person, the buyer generally must withhold 15% of the amount realized for the IRS, with reduced rates in some sales of homes the buyer will live in.
- **Tax reporting paperwork** so the closing agent can file Form 1099-S, or a certification that the sale of your main home is exempt from reporting
- **A bill of sale** for any personal property included

After the buyer signs and the lender funds, the closing agent pays off your mortgage, liens, and prorated taxes, pays the agreed costs and any credits, and sends you the balance.

## Deed recording and after closing

The closing agent records the new deed with the county recorder or register of deeds and pays any transfer taxes due under state or local law. Recording makes the transfer part of the public record.

Then wrap up:

- **Watch for your lien release.** Your lender should record a release or satisfaction of mortgage after payoff. Check the county records a few weeks later.
- **Collect your escrow refund.** If your servicer held money for taxes and insurance, any balance is refunded after payoff.
- **Cancel homeowners insurance** only after the sale closes and the buyer has possession.
- **Keep the settlement statement.** You'll need it for your tax return. If you owned and lived in the home for at least two of the five years before the sale, you can generally exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for most married couples filing jointly. [IRS Publication 523](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p523) has the details, and our [capital gains tax guide](/guides/capital-gains-tax-when-selling-your-home/) walks through examples.
