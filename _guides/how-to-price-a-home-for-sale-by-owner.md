This guide is for owners selling without a listing agent who want a list price they can defend to buyers, buyers' agents, and the appraiser. You'll learn how to choose comparable sales, do the price-per-square-foot math, adjust for real differences, and decide when to cut.

## Why pricing matters more when you sell by owner

Buyers and their agents expect owners to overprice. NAR's 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers found that only 5% of sellers sold without an agent, so a by-owner listing already gets extra scrutiny. If your price is out of line, buyers skip it, and agents steer their clients to listings they trust.

The other constraint is the appraisal. Most buyers finance, and the lender lends against the appraised value, not your contract price. If the appraisal comes in $15,000 short, you'll usually end up negotiating anyway, this time with less leverage and a closing date approaching. Price like an appraiser from the start and you avoid that fight.

## Step 1: Build a comp set the way an appraiser would

Comparable sales ("comps") are recent arm's-length sales of similar homes nearby. Fannie Mae's appraisal guidelines leave the choice of comps to the appraiser's judgment, but the preference is consistent: the most recent, the closest, and the most similar sales, with closed sales doing the heavy lifting.

1. **Start with closed sales from the past six months.** Stretch to twelve months only if your area is slow, and adjust older sales for market movement.
2. **Stay close.** Same subdivision first, then within about half a mile, staying in the same school district if you can. Don't cross a highway or a major road that changes the neighborhood.
3. **Match the basics.** Similar living area (within about 15%), similar age and style, and the same bed and bath count where possible.
4. **Skip non-arm's-length sales.** Foreclosures, sales between relatives, and estate sales at a discount distort value.
5. **Note pending and active listings separately.** Pending sales show where the market is heading. Active listings are your competition, and buyers will compare you with them.

Where to find the data: county assessor and recorder records (often searchable online), the sold history on public listing sites, a title company, or an appraiser. A flat-fee broker with MLS access can usually provide a comp report too; see [flat-fee MLS explained](/guides/flat-fee-mls-explained/).

## Step 2: Do the price-per-square-foot math

Divide each comp's sale price by its above-grade living area. Here's an example for a 1,950-square-foot, three-bedroom, two-bath house with an original kitchen and a two-car garage.

| Comp | Sale price | Sq ft | $/sq ft | Key differences | Example adjustment | Adjusted price |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A, sold 2 months ago | $412,000 | 2,000 | $206 | Renovated kitchen | −$15,000 | $397,000 |
| B, sold 3 months ago | $395,000 | 1,900 | $208 | One-car garage | +$8,000 | $403,000 |
| C, sold 1 month ago | $428,000 | 2,100 | $204 | Extra half bath, 150 sq ft larger | −$20,000 | $408,000 |
| D, sold 5 months ago | $389,000 | 1,850 | $210 | Older sale, 100 sq ft smaller | +$17,800 | $406,800 |

The raw average is about $207 per square foot, which points to roughly $403,650 (1,950 × $207). After adjustments, the comps cluster between $397,000 and $408,000. Give the most weight to the most recent and most similar sales (C and A here), and you land near $403,000. All of these numbers are illustrative.

### Where price per square foot misleads you

- **Size.** Price per square foot usually drops as homes get bigger, because the kitchen, baths, and lot don't double when the floor area does. Compare homes of similar size.
- **Below-grade space.** Finished basements are typically valued differently from above-grade rooms. Keep them out of the square footage and adjust separately.
- **Lot and location.** A corner lot on a busy road and a cul-de-sac lot backing onto woods can have identical houses and different values.

## Step 3: Adjust for the differences that matter

An adjustment answers one question: what would this comp have sold for if it matched your house? Add value when the comp is inferior to yours and subtract when it's superior. Typical adjustment items:

- Kitchen and bath condition (renovated vs. original)
- Bath count, especially a full bath vs. a half bath
- Garage spaces, pool, finished basement
- Lot size, view, and backing to a busy road
- Market movement since the comp closed

Adjustment amounts are local. One way to estimate them is a paired sale: two similar homes nearby where one difference explains most of the price gap. If two otherwise-similar houses sold for $395,000 and $410,000 and the only real difference was a renovated kitchen, that suggests about $15,000 for the kitchen in your market.

For market movement, compare the median price of similar sales now with six months ago, or check a public house price index for your metro. If prices rose 2% since Comp D closed, that's about $7,800 on a $389,000 sale.

## Step 4: Consider a pre-listing appraisal

A licensed appraiser will give you an independent opinion of value for a few hundred dollars in most markets. It's most useful when comps are thin, the home is unusual, or you and a co-owner disagree about price.

What it does well: it's a third-party number you can show buyers, and it flags problems a lender's appraiser would also notice. What it can't do: the buyer's lender won't use it, and it doesn't guarantee the buyer's appraisal matches. Tell the appraiser it's for listing purposes and ask for the comps they relied on.

## Step 5: Use pricing psychology without fooling yourself

**Price for search filters.** Buyers search in round bands: up to $350,000, $400,000, $450,000. A home at $399,900 appears in both the "up to $400,000" and "$350,000 and up" searches; at $405,000 it disappears from the first. If your indicated value sits just above a threshold, listing just under it often brings more showings and competing offers.

**Leave modest room, not a cushion.** Pricing 10% high "to negotiate" usually backfires: fewer showings, longer market time, and eventually a cut. A small margin is reasonable in a balanced market. In a hot one, pricing at or slightly below value can start a bidding contest.

**Separate price from credits.** Buyers often ask for closing-cost help. A $410,000 price with a $6,000 credit nets you about the same as $404,000 with no credit, but the house must still appraise at $410,000. Lenders also cap seller contributions. For conventional loans on a primary residence, Fannie Mae limits them to 3%, 6%, or 9% of value depending on the down payment. Check your number with the [seller net proceeds calculator](/tools/seller-net-proceeds-calculator/).

**Show your work.** Put a one-page comp sheet on the kitchen counter during showings. Buyers' agents respect a seller who prices from data, and it reduces lowball offers.

## Step 6: Read the market's response and adjust

Your first two to three weeks tell you whether the price is right. Here's how to read the signals:

| What you see | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Few online views or saves | Photos or price don't stand out in search | Improve photos first; then check the search band |
| Plenty of views, few showings | Price looks high next to competing listings | Compare with active listings; consider a cut |
| Many showings, no offers | Price or condition is out of line once buyers see it | Get feedback; fix issues or adjust price |
| Offers well below list | The market is telling you the value | Counter with comps; revisit your adjustments |
| Multiple offers in days | You priced at or below value | Set a deadline for best and final offers |

When you cut, make it count. One reduction that crosses a search threshold, for example from $415,000 to $399,900, reaches a new pool of buyers. Several $5,000 cuts mostly signal that the price is still too high.

## Pricing mistakes to avoid

- Using your purchase price plus improvements as the value. Buyers pay market value, not your cost.
- Pricing from a neighbor's asking price or an automated online estimate alone.
- Ignoring condition. A dated house can't be priced off renovated comps.
- Waiting too long to adjust while the listing goes stale.
