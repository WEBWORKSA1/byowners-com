This guide is for buyers who have found, or want to find, a home sold directly by its owner, with no listing agent on the other side. By the end you will know how to find by-owner homes, prepare your financing, choose the right level of professional help after the 2024 commission changes, write an offer with real protections, and get through inspection, appraisal, title, and closing safely.

## What changes when you buy directly from an owner

In a traditional sale, the listing agent coordinates disclosures, deadlines, and paperwork. In a by-owner sale, those jobs still exist; they just shift to you, the seller, and the professionals you hire.

| Task | Typical agent-listed sale | Direct-from-owner sale |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing guidance | Listing agent's market analysis | Your own comps, plus the lender's appraisal |
| Purchase contract | Agent-prepared state form | Your attorney, agent, or state-approved form |
| Earnest money | Held by the brokerage or title/escrow | Held by title, escrow, or attorney |
| Seller disclosures | Collected by the listing agent | Request them directly from the seller |
| Deadlines and scheduling | Agents coordinate | You, your agent or attorney, and the title company |
| Closing | Title company or attorney | Title company or attorney |

The upside is direct communication with the person who knows the house best, and a seller who is not paying a listing commission may have more room to negotiate. The tradeoff is that nobody on the other side is managing the process, so your organization matters.

## Find by-owner homes

Start with dedicated by-owner listings, such as [ByOwners.com listings](/listings/), and set alerts for your target neighborhoods. Then work the offline channels many sellers still use: yard signs, community boards, neighborhood groups, and local papers. Some buyers also mail polite letters to owners in a specific neighborhood.

Before you get attached, confirm the person selling actually owns the home. Look up the property in the county assessor or recorder records and check that the owner's name matches the seller's ID. Seller-impersonation fraud, especially on vacant land and unoccupied homes, is a real risk.

## Get pre-approved and organize your money

A by-owner seller cannot see your file through an agent, so show them you are real:

- **Pre-approval letter.** A lender has reviewed your credit, income, and assets, not just your stated numbers. It is far stronger than a pre-qualification.
- **Proof of funds** for your down payment and closing costs, such as a redacted bank statement.
- **A closing-cost estimate**, so you know your cash to close before you negotiate.

Plan for earnest money held by a neutral title company, escrow agent, or closing attorney, never wired to the seller. Before any wire, call the title company at a number you found independently, not one from an email, to confirm instructions. Wire fraud targets home buyers precisely at this step.

## Choose your level of help after the 2024 changes

Under the settlement involving the National Association of REALTORS®, practice changes took effect August 17, 2024. According to [NAR's announcement](https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/national-association-of-realtors-reminds-members-and-consumers-of-real-estate-practice-change), agents who are MLS participants must have a **written agreement** with a buyer before touring a home, and offers of buyer-agent compensation can no longer be posted on the MLS. Compensation is still allowed; it is simply negotiated and spelled out in writing. Your options:

- **Full-service buyer's agent.** Sign a written agreement that states the fee. You can ask the seller to pay part or all of it as a term of your offer, but a by-owner seller is not obligated to agree, so know what you will owe if they decline.
- **Limited or flat-fee help.** Some agents will write the offer or handle negotiation for a set fee. The agreement still has to be in writing.
- **Real estate attorney.** In many areas an attorney can draft or review the contract, clear title issues, and conduct or oversee the closing for a flat or hourly fee.
- **Do it yourself** with a state-approved purchase form and a title or escrow company. This works best for experienced buyers on straightforward deals.

Read the agreement before you tour. Under the new rules, the agreement must spell out the compensation your agent will receive, your agent cannot be paid more than that amount from any source, and the terms are negotiable. The [NAR settlement FAQ](https://nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2024-10/nar-settlement-faq-2024-10-29.pdf) and state regulators such as the [North Carolina Real Estate Commission](https://bulletins.ncrec.gov/has-the-world-exploded-the-nar-settlement-commission-law-and-rules/) cover the details.

## Write an offer that protects you

Your purchase agreement should name the parties and property (legal description), the price, earnest money amount and holder, financing terms, closing date, what conveys (appliances, fixtures), who pays which closing costs, and the deadline for the seller to respond. Then add contingencies, each with a hard date:

| Contingency | What it protects | Example deadline |
|---|---|---|
| Inspection | Cancel or renegotiate after professional inspections | 7–10 days after acceptance |
| Financing | Recover earnest money if your loan is denied | 21–30 days |
| Appraisal | Renegotiate or cancel if the home appraises low | Tied to the appraisal report |
| Title | Cancel if title has defects the seller cannot cure | Before closing |
| Seller disclosures | Review required disclosures before you are bound | A few days after receipt |
| HOA documents | Review rules, budget, and fees | Several days after receipt |

The deadlines are examples; local custom and your lender's timeline should drive yours. Most states require sellers to give buyers a property condition disclosure, so ask for it before you write the offer. Our guide to [seller disclosure requirements](/guides/seller-disclosure-requirements/) explains what owners typically must reveal.

## Inspection, appraisal, and title

**Inspection.** Hire a licensed home inspector and attend the inspection. Add specialists based on the house: sewer-line camera for older homes, radon testing, wood-destroying insect inspection, roof, chimney, septic and well testing for rural properties. For homes built before 1978, federal rules give buyers an opportunity, typically 10 days, to test for lead-based paint unless you waive it, and the seller must provide the EPA lead pamphlet and disclose known hazards under the [EPA disclosure rule](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-based-paint-disclosure-rule-section-1018-title-x). Use the inspection report to request repairs, a credit, or a price reduction, in writing, before the contingency expires.

**Appraisal.** Your lender orders it, and you usually pay for it. If it comes in low, your appraisal contingency gives you options: the seller can reduce the price, you can cover the gap, you can meet in the middle, or you can walk away with your deposit.

**Title.** The title company or attorney searches public records for liens, unpaid taxes, judgments, easements, and ownership gaps. Buy an **owner's title insurance** policy; the lender's policy protects only the lender. Order a survey if boundary lines, fences, or additions look questionable.

## Closing: attorney states and escrow states

Closing customs vary. In some states, attorneys conduct closings; in others, title or escrow companies handle everything and attorneys are optional. Either way, the closing agent collects funds, pays off the seller's mortgage, records the deed, and issues title insurance.

If you are financing, your lender must give you a Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing. Compare it line by line with your Loan Estimate. Do a final walk-through within 24 hours of closing to confirm agreed repairs were made, nothing that conveys was removed, and the home is in the condition you agreed to. Rules vary by state, so confirm the process with your closing attorney or title company.

## Red flags in a by-owner deal

1. The seller's name does not match county ownership records.
2. Pressure to wire money directly to the seller or skip a title company.
3. Refusal to allow an inspection, or insistence on an "as-is, no inspection" deal with no price justification.
4. No written disclosures in a state that requires them.
5. Additions, finished basements, or garage conversions with no permits on file.
6. A price far below comparable sales with an urgent deadline.
7. Liens, judgments, or a recent foreclosure filing in public records.
8. A seller who will not sign a written contract or wants side agreements off the record.

None of these automatically kill a deal, but each needs a clear answer, in writing, before you go further.
