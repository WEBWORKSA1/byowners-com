This guide is for anyone selling a car, truck, SUV, or motorcycle privately instead of trading it in. When you finish it, you'll know how to price the vehicle, clear the title, fill out the right paperwork, run safe test drives, and get paid without falling for the scams that target private sellers.

## Price it like a buyer would

Private-party value usually sits between trade-in value and dealer retail, and that gap is the reason to sell it yourself. To find your number:

- Check two or three online pricing guides using your exact year, trim, mileage, options, and honest condition.
- Search local listings for the same model within a reasonable radius, and note how long similar cars have been listed.
- Adjust for what buyers actually pay for: service records, matching tires, a clean history, and recent big-ticket maintenance like brakes or a timing belt.

Price a little above your walk-away number so you can say yes to a reasonable counteroffer. If you've had no serious inquiries within a week or two, the market is telling you something.

Before you list, run a title check through an approved **NMVTIS** provider (the Justice Department's National Motor Vehicle Title Information System). It shows title brands like salvage or flood and the last reported odometer reading. Serious buyers will run one, so you want to know what they'll see.

## Get your title ready and handle any loan

The title is what legally transfers ownership, so sort it out before you advertise.

### If you own the car outright

Find the physical title and check that the names, VIN, and address are correct. If two owners are listed with "and," both usually need to sign. If the title is lost, apply for a duplicate now; it can take weeks.

### If you still have a loan

Call your lender for a **payoff letter** showing the exact amount and how long the quote is good. You have three common options:

1. Pay off the loan yourself before the sale and wait for the lien release and title.
2. Close the deal at your lender's branch, where the buyer's payment goes straight to the loan and the remainder to you.
3. Use a written agreement where the buyer pays the lender directly, then you receive the balance, with the title released to the buyer once the lien clears.

Many lenders now hold titles electronically, so ask how long the release takes in your state. Buyers are rightly nervous about paying for a car whose title they can't see, and a clear payoff plan calms them down.

## The paperwork: bill of sale, odometer disclosure, and state forms

### Bill of sale

Even where it's not required, a **bill of sale** is your proof of what happened. Include:

- Buyer's and seller's full names and addresses
- Year, make, model, and VIN
- Sale price and date
- Odometer reading at sale
- A statement that the vehicle is sold **as is**, with no warranty
- Signatures of both parties

Some states have their own bill of sale form or require notarization, so check your DMV's website first.

### Odometer disclosure

Federal law requires the seller to disclose mileage when ownership transfers, usually on the title itself. Under the rule NHTSA updated in 2019, **model year 2011 and newer vehicles** require a disclosure on every transfer for their first 20 years; a 2011 model, for example, doesn't become exempt until January 1, 2031. **Model year 2010 and older** vehicles remain under the old 10-year rule, so they're exempt today.

Write the exact reading with no tenths, and if you know the odometer is wrong or has rolled over, check the box that says so. Knowingly misstating mileage to defraud a buyer violates federal law, so treat this line as seriously as the price.

## Showing the car and running safe test drives

Screen buyers by message first. Ask what they're looking for, whether they've seen your listing details, and how they plan to pay. Vague answers and pushy timelines are red flags.

For the meeting:

- Choose a busy public place in daylight. Many police departments offer a designated safe exchange zone.
- Bring a friend, and tell someone else where you'll be.
- Before any test drive, photograph the buyer's driver's license and ask to see proof of insurance.
- Ride along. Never hand over the keys and let a stranger drive off alone.
- If the buyer wants a pre-purchase inspection, agree to it, but you drive the car to their mechanic.

## Getting paid without getting scammed

Payment is where private sellers lose the most money. The FTC has warned about a scam aimed squarely at online car sellers: a remote "buyer" sends a check for more than your price, then asks you to send back the difference, often to pay a shipper. The check looks real and the funds appear in your account, but the bank later discovers it's fake, reverses the deposit, and you owe the bank everything you sent.

| Payment method | Main risk | How to use it safely |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | Counterfeit bills, carrying large sums | Meet at your bank and have a teller verify and deposit it on the spot |
| Cashier's check | Convincing fakes | Go with the buyer to their bank and watch it get issued; otherwise call the issuing bank at a number from its official website, not the one printed on the check |
| Bank or wire transfer | Fake "payment sent" screenshots or emails | Confirm the funds in your own account through your bank's app or branch before signing the title |
| Payment apps | Transfer limits, fake payment notifications, disputed transactions | Fine for a small deposit; avoid for the full price of a vehicle |
| Escrow service | Fake escrow websites | Only use a service you find and verify yourself, never a link the buyer sends |
| Personal check | Bounces after you've handed over the title | Don't accept one |

Two rules cover nearly every scam: never accept more than your asking price, and never send money back to a buyer for any reason. The FBI has also warned car shoppers about fake "buyer protection programs" that ask for payment in gift cards. If you're buying your next car privately, a seller who won't meet in person or let you inspect the vehicle is a warning sign.

## Close the deal step by step

When you've agreed on a price and verified the payment method, follow this order on sale day:

1. Confirm the funds are in your account, or that the teller has verified and deposited the cash.
2. Complete and sign the bill of sale; give the buyer a copy and keep one.
3. Fill in the odometer disclosure and sign the title exactly where your state's title shows, with no corrections or white-out.
4. Hand over the title, keys, manuals, spare key, and service records.
5. Remove your plates if your state requires it, plus toll transponders and parking permits.
6. Sign out of the car's infotainment system and connected-car app, unpair your phone, and clear stored addresses and garage door codes.
7. File your release of liability or notice of sale with the DMV the same day.
8. Call your insurer to remove the car after the transfer is complete, not before.

## After the sale: release of liability and plates

Most states have a way to tell the DMV you no longer own the vehicle. In California, for example, you must submit a **Notice of Transfer and Release of Liability** within five calendar days of the sale, either online or on the paper form. It doesn't transfer ownership, which only happens when the buyer submits the endorsed title, but it protects you from parking tickets, tolls, and liability for what the buyer does with the car afterward.

Plates follow different rules from state to state. In many states they stay with you to transfer or surrender; in others they stay on the car. Never let a buyer drive away on your registration unless your state specifically allows it.

## Smog and safety inspections

Some states require an emissions or safety inspection at transfer. California's rules are a good example of how specific they get: the seller generally must provide a valid smog certification, and a private-party sale needs one from within the last 90 days. Gasoline and hybrid vehicles four or fewer model years old are exempt, and the buyer pays a smog transfer fee instead. Diesel vehicles get no model-year exemption.

Other states require nothing at sale, or put the inspection on the buyer at registration. Rules vary by state, so check your DMV or state emissions program before you list so a failed test doesn't stall the deal.
