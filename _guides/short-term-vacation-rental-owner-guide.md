This guide is for owners who rent a second home, a cabin, a condo, or part of their primary residence to travelers by the night or week, and who want to do it legally and profitably without a management company. By the end you will know which permissions to secure, how lodging taxes and IRS personal-use rules work, how to set seasonal prices, and how to run turnovers, guest screening, and insurance like a professional host.

## Start with permission: zoning, permits, HOA rules, and your loan

Short-term rental rules are local and change often, so verify them before you buy furniture. Work through this list:

1. **Zoning.** Confirm that short-term rentals, often defined as stays under 30 days, are allowed in your zone. Some cities allow them only in commercial or tourist districts, or only in an owner's primary residence.
2. **Permit or registration.** Many jurisdictions require a permit number that must appear on every listing, plus annual renewal fees.
3. **Operating rules.** Look for caps on nights per year, occupancy limits per bedroom, parking minimums, quiet hours, trash rules, and a requirement for a local contact who answers complaints within a set time.
4. **Safety inspection.** Expect smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, fire extinguishers, egress windows, and posted emergency information.
5. **HOA or condo documents.** Many associations ban rentals shorter than 30 days or a set number of months. HOA rules can be stricter than city law, and they are enforceable against you.
6. **Your mortgage.** Second-home loans typically limit rental arrangements, and investment-property loans have their own terms. Read your loan documents and talk to your lender before you list.

Older homes have one bit of good news: the federal lead-based paint disclosure rule exempts leases of 100 days or less where no renewal or extension can occur, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-based-paint-disclosure-rule-section-1018-title-x). Local safety codes still apply.

## Lodging and occupancy taxes

Most places treat short stays as lodging, like a hotel room, and tax them. Depending on location, you may owe:

- State sales tax on short-term lodging
- A city or county **transient occupancy tax** (also called a hotel, lodging, or room tax)
- A tourism or special-district assessment

Register with each taxing authority before your first stay, and collect the tax from guests as part of the booking total. Some booking channels collect and remit certain state or local taxes on your behalf, but rarely all of them, and you remain responsible for any that are missed. Keep a spreadsheet by month showing gross rent, cleaning fees, taxes collected, and taxes remitted by channel.

Example: on a $1,500 stay in a jurisdiction with a combined 12% lodging tax, the guest pays $180 in tax, which you or the booking channel remit. Rates and taxable items (cleaning fees, pet fees) vary, so check each authority's rules.

## Federal income tax: how personal use changes everything

The IRS sorts vacation properties by how much you use them personally. Under [IRS Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415) and [Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527), you use a dwelling unit **as a home** if your personal-use days exceed the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days it is rented at a fair rental price.

| Your situation | General federal tax treatment |
|---|---|
| Used as a home and rented fewer than 15 days | Do not report the rental income; do not deduct rental expenses (mortgage interest and property taxes may still be itemized) |
| Used as a home and rented 15 days or more | Report all rental income; divide expenses between rental and personal use; rental deductions cannot exceed rental income |
| Personal use within the 14-day or 10% limit | Treated as a rental; report income and deduct allocated expenses, and a loss may be possible subject to other limits |

How the 10% test plays out: rent the home 204 nights at fair rental price and you can use it up to 20 days without crossing the line. Rent it 90 nights and the limit is 14 days, because 10% of 90 is less than 14. Personal-use days include days used by you or family members, days donated or swapped, and days rented to anyone below a fair rental price. Days spent mainly doing repairs and maintenance generally do not count.

Two more rules matter for short stays:

- **The 7-day average.** If the average guest stay is 7 days or less, the activity generally is not a "rental activity" under the passive activity rules in [IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925). That changes how losses are treated, and whether you materially participate becomes important.
- **Schedule C vs. Schedule E.** [IRS Publication 334](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334) says owners who provide hotel-style services for guests, such as maid service, report on Schedule C. Most owners who provide only the space and cleaning between guests use Schedule E, but the line is fact-specific and can add self-employment tax.

These rules interact in ways that change your tax bill, so meet with a tax professional before your first full year.

## Pricing and seasonality

Start with a **base nightly rate** from comparable properties near you with the same sleeping capacity, then layer adjustments:

- **Season.** Set distinct peak, shoulder, and off-peak rates.
- **Day of week.** Charge weekend premiums in leisure markets; in business-travel areas, weekdays may be stronger.
- **Lead time.** Hold firm far out in peak season; cut modestly on open nights within the last two weeks.
- **Minimum stays.** Require longer minimums in peak season and shorten them to fill gaps between bookings.
- **Fees.** Price the cleaning fee to cover your actual turnover cost, and disclose every mandatory fee up front.

Here is an illustrative year for a three-bedroom home in a summer-peak market:

| Season (example) | Nights available | Nightly rate | Occupancy | Booked nights | Rent revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peak (Jun–Aug) | 92 | $325 | 85% | 78 | $25,350 |
| Shoulder (Apr–May, Sep–Oct) | 122 | $225 | 60% | 73 | $16,425 |
| Off-peak (Nov–Mar) | 151 | $160 | 35% | 53 | $8,480 |
| **Year** | **365** | | **56%** | **204** | **$50,255** |

Judge results by **revenue per available night** (here, about $138) rather than occupancy alone. A full calendar at too low a rate means more wear and more turnovers for less money. Before you budget, subtract cleaning, supplies, utilities, internet, booking fees, lodging taxes, insurance, maintenance, and furniture replacement. ## Cleaning and turnover systems

Turnovers are the operational heart of a vacation rental. Set a checkout time, such as 10 or 11 a.m., and a check-in time no earlier than 4 p.m. so the cleaner has a realistic window. Keep three sets of linens per bed so one is on the bed, one is in the wash, and one is on the shelf. Each turnover should follow a written checklist:

1. Photograph the home on arrival and report any damage the same day.
2. Strip and remake beds; wash and fold linens and towels.
3. Clean kitchen, bathrooms, floors, and high-touch surfaces.
4. Empty the refrigerator, dishwasher, and all trash.
5. Restock toilet paper, soap, coffee, and basics to set quantities.
6. Check smoke and CO alarms, lights, remotes, Wi-Fi, and thermostat settings.
7. Reset furniture to staged positions and lock up.

Pay your cleaner promptly and keep a backup cleaner. A missed turnover in peak season costs a booking and a review.

## Guest screening and house rules

You cannot interview every guest, but you can set conditions that filter out problems:

- Verify the booking guest's identity and require that they are on the stay.
- Use a rental agreement with an occupancy limit, a no-parties rule, quiet hours, pet terms, and a damage policy.
- Collect a security deposit or use a damage protection program, and state how claims work.
- Use noise-level monitors that measure decibels without recording audio, and disclose any exterior cameras. Never place cameras inside sleeping areas or bathrooms; many state and local laws restrict cameras in rentals.

Screen on conduct and verifiable information, never on who the guest is. Anti-discrimination laws can apply to lodging, and booking channels have their own non-discrimination policies. Short-term rentals also attract fraud; see our guide to [avoiding scams when buying, selling, or renting by owner](/guides/avoid-scams-buying-selling-renting-by-owner/).

## Insurance for short-term rentals

Many homeowners policies exclude or limit coverage for business use of the home, which can include renting to paying guests. Ask your agent four direct questions and get the answers in writing:

1. Does my policy cover the structure and contents while guests are staying?
2. What liability coverage applies if a guest is injured?
3. Is lost rental income covered after a covered loss?
4. Does my umbrella policy exclude business or rental activity?

Most owners need a short-term rental endorsement or a vacation-rental policy. Host protection programs offered by booking channels have limits and exclusions, and they are not a substitute for your own coverage.
