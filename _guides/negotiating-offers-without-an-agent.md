This guide is for owners selling without a listing agent who have an offer in hand, or expect one soon, and want to negotiate it like a professional. You'll learn how to read every term that matters, compare offers by what you actually keep, write counteroffers, and handle buyers' agents and multiple offers.

## Read the offer: the terms that matter

A purchase offer is a proposed contract. The price gets your attention, but the other terms decide whether the deal closes and how much you walk away with.

| Term | What to check | Red flags |
|---|---|---|
| Price | How it compares with your closed comps | Well above comps on a financed offer (appraisal risk) |
| Financing | Cash, conventional, FHA, or VA; down payment; lender pre-approval | No pre-approval letter; unknown lender; tiny down payment on a stretched price |
| Earnest money | Amount, deadline to deposit, and who holds it | Deposit paid to you directly; vague holder; long deposit deadline |
| Contingencies | Inspection, appraisal, financing, sale of the buyer's home | Open-ended deadlines; a home-sale contingency with no kick-out clause |
| Closing and possession | Closing date; when you move out | A date that doesn't fit your next home; rent-back terms left undefined |
| Credits and fees | Closing-cost credit; request that you pay the buyer's agent | Large credits stacked on a top-dollar price |
| Inclusions | Appliances, fixtures, window treatments | Items you planned to take |
| Expiration | When the offer lapses | Pressure to respond within a couple of hours |

The earnest money should go to a neutral holder, such as the title company, escrow agent, or closing attorney, never to you. That protects both sides and makes the contract's default terms enforceable.

## Compare offers by net and certainty

Translate every offer into the dollars you'll keep and the risk you'll carry. Here's an example with two offers on the same house:

| Term | Offer A | Offer B |
|---|---|---|
| Price | $415,000 | $405,000 |
| Financing | FHA, 3.5% down | Conventional, 20% down |
| Earnest money | $4,000 | $15,000 |
| Closing-cost credit requested | $8,000 | None |
| Buyer's agent fee requested | 2.5% ($10,375) | None; buyer is unrepresented |
| Contingencies | 14-day inspection, appraisal, financing, sale of buyer's home | 7-day inspection, financing; buyer covers up to $10,000 of any appraisal gap |
| Closing | 45 days | 30 days |
| **Price minus credits and fees** | **$396,625** | **$405,000** |

Offer B nets $8,375 more, closes sooner, and carries less risk: a bigger deposit, a shorter inspection window, appraisal-gap coverage, and no home-sale contingency. Offer A isn't worthless, but you'd counter it before accepting. Run your own scenarios in the [seller net proceeds calculator](/tools/seller-net-proceeds-calculator/).

## What the buyer's financing means for you

**Cash.** Ask for proof of funds, such as a recent bank or brokerage statement. Cash buyers usually close faster and may skip the appraisal, but confirm the contract says so.

**Conventional loans.** The lender orders an appraisal and caps how much the seller can contribute. For a primary residence, Fannie Mae limits seller contributions to 3%, 6%, or 9% of value depending on the loan-to-value ratio, and 2% for investment properties. A credit above the cap has to be restructured, usually as a lower price.

**FHA and VA loans.** These programs have their own appraisal and property condition standards, and their contracts include a required clause that lets the buyer walk away if the appraisal comes in below the price. They also set their own limits on seller concessions. Peeling paint or a missing handrail can become a required repair before closing.

**Verify the pre-approval.** A pre-approval letter is only as good as the lender behind it. With the buyer's permission, call the loan officer and ask whether the buyer's income, assets, and credit have been documented and whether the appraisal has been ordered.

Remember the federal timing rule: lenders must give buyers the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing, and certain late changes to the loan can restart that clock. A closing date with no cushion invites delays.

## Write counteroffers that keep the deal moving

A counteroffer rejects the buyer's offer and proposes new terms. Do it in writing every time.

1. **Thank the buyer and acknowledge the offer.** Tone matters even on paper.
2. **Change only what matters.** Price, credits, deadlines, and contingencies are fair game; nitpicking every term drags things out.
3. **Trade, don't just take.** If you want more money, offer something back: a quicker closing, a longer rent-back, or included appliances.
4. **Shorten loose deadlines.** Tighten inspection and loan-approval periods and add a kick-out clause to any home-sale contingency, letting you keep marketing and giving the buyer, say, 72 hours to remove the contingency if you get another offer.
5. **Set an expiration,** usually 24 to 48 hours.
6. **Sign and send through one channel** so there's a clean record of each version.

## Handle multiple offers

Tell every interested buyer that you've received multiple offers, and set a deadline for "highest and best" terms. Then compare with the same net-and-certainty table.

- **Escalation clauses.** A buyer offers to beat any competing offer by a set amount, up to a cap. Require a copy of the competing offer before the escalation applies, and make sure the escalated price still appraises.
- **Countering more than one buyer.** If you counter several buyers at once, state in writing that no counter is binding until you sign a final acceptance. Otherwise two buyers could both accept and you'd have two contracts.
- **Backup offers.** Keep the runner-up as a signed backup that becomes active only if the first contract terminates.

## When the buyer has an agent

Since August 17, 2024, agents working with buyers must have a written agreement before touring a home, and offers of compensation to buyers' agents can't be published in the MLS. In practice, many offers you receive will include a request that you pay some or all of the buyer's agent fee.

Treat it like any other term. You can agree, offer a smaller amount, convert it to a closing-cost credit, or decline. Whatever you agree to should be written into the purchase contract, not settled by a side email. The buyer's agent represents the buyer, not you, so keep conversations in writing and never reveal your bottom line. For the background, see [real estate commission rule changes explained](/guides/real-estate-commission-rule-changes-explained/).

## Renegotiation after inspection and appraisal

Signing the contract starts round two.

**Inspection.** Expect a repair request. Your options are to repair, give a credit, reduce the price, or decline. Credits are often cleanest, because you avoid managing contractors on the buyer's schedule, but lender caps on seller contributions still apply. For a home built before 1978, federal rules give the buyer a 10-day opportunity for a lead inspection unless they agree in writing to change or waive it.

**Appraisal.** If a financed buyer's appraisal comes in low, the usual paths are to lower the price, have the buyer pay the gap in cash, split it, or let the buyer cancel under the appraisal contingency. Ask for a copy of the appraisal and check the comps it used; a clear error can sometimes be challenged through the lender.

## Habits that protect your position

- **Never negotiate during a showing.** Thank the buyer and say you'll respond in writing.
- **Know your walk-away number** before the first offer arrives: your loan payoff, estimated closing costs, and the net you need for your next move.
- **Treat a lowball offer as information, not an insult.** Counter with your comp sheet instead of ignoring it.
- **Keep your reasons for selling private.** A job transfer or divorce deadline tells the other side you're under time pressure.
- **Put every change in a signed amendment.** Contracts for real estate generally must be in writing to be enforceable, so a handshake promise to fix the deck is worth nothing at closing.

## Use an attorney for the contract

Rules vary by state, so confirm the details with a local real estate attorney. Many charge a flat fee to review or draft a purchase agreement, and in attorney-closing states you'll need one anyway. A good review checks that deadlines are specific dates, contingencies have exits that protect you, the earnest money terms are clear, the lead and state disclosures are attached, and the default remedies are spelled out. That review costs a small fraction of what one bad clause can cost you.
