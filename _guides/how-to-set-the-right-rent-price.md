This guide is for owners renting out a house, condo, or small multifamily unit who want a rent number they can defend: high enough to cover costs and earn a return, low enough to lease quickly to a qualified tenant. You will learn how to build and adjust rental comps, where the 1% rule helps and where it misleads, how to do the vacancy math, and how to price renewals.

## Start with what the market is actually paying

Tenants do not care what your mortgage costs. Your rent is set by the homes they are comparing yours to, so begin with comparable rentals, or **comps**:

- **Same type and size.** Match property type (single-family, townhome, condo, duplex unit), bedroom and bathroom count, and square footage within about 15%.
- **Same area.** Stay within about a mile, and inside the same school attendance zone if families are your likely tenants.
- **Recent.** Use homes listed in the last 60 to 90 days. Note how long each one sat; a listing that disappears in a week was priced right or low, and one that lingers for six weeks was priced high.
- **Leased, not just listed.** Asking rents are wishes. If a local property manager or another owner will tell you what similar homes actually leased for, weight that number most.

Aim for four to six solid comps. Fewer than three, and you are guessing; more than eight, and you are probably including homes that are not truly comparable.

## Adjust comps like an appraiser

No comp is identical to your home, so adjust each comp's rent up or down to account for differences. Add value when the comp lacks a feature your home has; subtract when the comp has something yours lacks. The dollar values below are illustrative only; derive yours by comparing similar local listings that differ by a single feature.

| Feature difference | Example monthly adjustment |
|---|---|
| Extra bedroom | $100–$250 |
| Extra full bathroom | $50–$150 |
| Garage vs. street parking | $50–$125 |
| In-unit washer and dryer | $40–$100 |
| Fenced yard | $25–$75 |
| Updated kitchen and baths vs. dated | $75–$200 |
| Water, sewer, and trash included | Roughly what those bills run locally |

**Worked example.** Your home is a three-bedroom, two-bath house with a garage and a dated kitchen. Comp A, an identical home with an updated kitchen, leased at $2,150; subtract $100, so $2,050. Comp B, a three-bedroom with one bath and no garage, leased at $1,875; add $100 for the bath and $75 for the garage, so $2,050. Comp C, a similar home that sat for five weeks at $2,250, tells you the ceiling is below $2,250. Your realistic range is about $2,000 to $2,100.

## Use the 1% rule as a screen, not a price

The **1% rule** is an investor shortcut: if monthly rent is at least 1% of the purchase price, the property may be worth a closer look. On a $250,000 home, that means $2,500 a month.

It is useful for one thing, quickly sorting potential purchases. It is useless for setting rent on a home you already own, for several reasons:

- In many higher-cost metro areas, few homes come close to 1%, while some lower-cost markets exceed it easily.
- It ignores property taxes, insurance, HOA dues, and maintenance, which vary enormously.
- It tells you nothing about what a tenant will pay. If the rule says $2,500 and your comps say $2,050, the market wins, and a listing at $2,500 will sit empty.

## Do the vacancy math before you hold out for top dollar

Owners overprice because the cost of a higher rent feels small and the cost of vacancy feels abstract. Put both in dollars. Using an example $2,000 home, each vacant day costs about $67 in rent before you add utilities, lawn care, and mortgage payments you are covering alone.

| Pricing choice (example) | Monthly rent | Months vacant | Rent collected over 12 months |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hold out at the top | $2,050 | 2 | $20,500 |
| Price at the comps | $2,000 | 1 | $22,000 |
| Price slightly under | $1,950 | 0.5 | $22,425 |

The break-even is simple: lowering rent by $50 costs $600 over a 12-month lease. If the lower price fills the home about nine days sooner, it pays for itself, and anything faster is profit. Under IRS rules, ordinary expenses for a vacant home you are actively holding out for rent are generally still deductible, according to [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527), but a deduction only softens a loss you could have avoided.

## Read the market's feedback in the first two weeks

Your listing performance is real-time pricing data. In a typical market, a well-priced home with good photos should produce steady inquiries in the first few days and showings within the first week. Use this sequence:

1. Track inquiries, showings, and applications daily from the day you list.
2. If inquiries are strong but no one applies after viewing, the home is not matching the photos or the price is slightly high for the condition.
3. If inquiries are thin after 7 to 10 days, recheck photos and description, then cut the price in one meaningful step rather than several small ones.
4. Price just under round numbers that renters use as search filters, such as $1,995 instead of $2,025, so you appear in more searches.

Timing matters too. In most markets, more households move in late spring and summer, so a lease that ends in winter can mean a longer, cheaper vacancy. When you sign, consider a lease term, such as 15 months instead of 12, that ends in a stronger month.

## Price renewals to keep good tenants

A reliable tenant who pays on time and treats the home well is your most valuable asset. Before you raise the rent, estimate the true cost of turnover:

| Turnover cost (example, $2,000 rent) | Amount |
|---|---|
| One month of vacancy | $2,000 |
| Cleaning | $400 |
| Paint touch-up and minor repairs | $900 |
| Marketing, screening time, and utilities while vacant | $300 |
| **Total** | **$3,600** |

A $100 monthly increase brings in $1,200 a year. If there is even a modest chance it causes a good tenant to leave, the expected cost can wipe out the gain. Many experienced landlords raise rent modestly every year, close to the market, rather than holding it flat for years and then imposing a large catch-up increase.

Check the law before you send a renewal notice. States set minimum notice periods for increases, some states (including California and Oregon) cap annual increases for many units, and a number of cities have rent control or stabilization. Fair housing rules also apply to pricing: increases must be applied on the same basis to everyone, never adjusted for who the tenant is. Our guide to [fair housing rules for private landlords](/guides/fair-housing-rules-for-private-landlords/) explains the protected classes.

## Pricing with housing vouchers and family tenants

If you accept Housing Choice Voucher tenants, the local housing agency will review your asking rent against similar unassisted units before approving the lease, so a rent supported by comps moves faster. As of January 2025, 23 states and the District of Columbia had statewide laws making source of income a protected class, according to [HUD's Office of Inspector General](https://www.hudoig.gov/reports-publications/report/public-housing-authorities-and-source-income-discrimination), and many cities and counties add their own protections. In those places, refusing voucher holders or setting a different price for them can be illegal.

Renting to a relative at a discount has a tax cost. Days rented to anyone at less than a fair rental price count as personal-use days under [IRS Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415), which can limit your deductions.
