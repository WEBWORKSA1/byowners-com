This guide is for buyers who cannot or would rather not use a bank loan, and for sellers considering carrying the financing on a home they own. You will learn the three common structures, see a worked amortization example with a balloon payment, understand the federal rules sellers face, and know how to avoid the two biggest traps: due-on-sale clauses and balloon refinancing risk.

## How owner financing works

In owner financing, also called **seller financing**, the seller extends credit to the buyer for part of the purchase price. Instead of sending a bank a monthly payment, the buyer pays the seller, or better, a loan servicing company that pays the seller.

It shows up most often when:

- The seller owns the home free and clear and wants steady income.
- The buyer has a sizable down payment but nontraditional income, such as self-employment.
- The property is hard to finance conventionally, such as land, a home needing repairs, or a unique rural property.
- Interest rates are high and the seller can offer terms that make the deal work.

## The three common structures

| Structure | Who holds title | Buyer's position | If the buyer defaults |
|---|---|---|---|
| Promissory note plus mortgage or deed of trust | Buyer, at closing | Owns the home; seller holds a recorded lien | Seller forecloses under state law |
| Land contract (contract for deed) | Seller, until paid in full | Equitable interest; deed comes later | Forfeiture or foreclosure, depending on state law |
| Lease-option | Seller, until the option is exercised | Tenant with a right to buy at a set price | Lease remedies; option fee and credits usually lost |

**Note plus mortgage or deed of trust** is the cleanest structure for most deals. The buyer gets the deed, the seller's security interest is recorded, and both sides have well-tested legal rules.

**Land contracts** let a seller keep title as security, which sounds safer for the seller but can create problems for the buyer: liens against the seller, a seller who dies or will not deliver the deed, and in some states fast forfeiture of years of payments. Several states now regulate them closely.

**Lease-options** combine a lease with an option to buy, usually for an upfront option fee and sometimes a portion of each rent payment credited toward the price. They suit buyers who need time to repair credit, but the buyer is a tenant until closing and builds no legal ownership.

## Worked example: payments, interest, and a balloon

Here is an illustrative deal. A home sells for $300,000. The buyer puts down $30,000, and the seller finances $270,000 at a 7% fixed rate, amortized over 30 years with a **balloon** due after five years.

The monthly principal and interest payment is **$1,796.32**. Here is how the balance moves:

| Payment | Interest | Principal | Remaining balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Month 1 | $1,575.00 | $221.32 | $269,778.68 |
| Month 12 | $1,560.38 | $235.94 | $267,257.31 |
| Month 60 | $1,484.39 | $311.92 | $254,155.29 |

Over five years the buyer pays about $107,779, of which roughly $91,934 is interest and $15,845 is principal. At month 60, the buyer owes a balloon payment of about **$254,155**, which in practice means refinancing with a bank or selling.

For comparison, the same loan fully amortized over 15 years would cost about $2,426.84 a month, and an interest-only structure would cost $1,575 a month while paying down nothing. Run your own numbers with the [mortgage calculator](/tools/mortgage-calculator/), and add property taxes and insurance, which the buyer still owes.

## Dodd-Frank and the SAFE Act: what sellers should know

After the 2008 crisis, federal law tightened the rules for anyone who originates home loans, and seller-financers are not automatically exempt. This is a general overview; confirm the current text with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) and a real estate attorney before you commit.

**Loan originator rules.** Regulation Z, [12 CFR 1026.36](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1026/subpart-E/section-1026.36), contains two exclusions for sellers who finance homes they own, as adopted in the [CFPB's 2013 final rule](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201301_cfpb_final-rule_loan-originator-compensation-amendments.pdf):

- **One-property exclusion.** Available to a natural person, estate, or trust financing only one property in any 12-month period. The seller must own the property and must not have built the home as a builder in the ordinary course of business. The loan cannot have negative amortization and must have a fixed rate, or an adjustable rate that stays fixed for at least five years with reasonable caps. A balloon is not prohibited under this exclusion.
- **Three-property exclusion.** Available to any person or entity financing no more than three properties in any 12-month period, with the same ownership and construction conditions. The loan must be **fully amortizing**, so no balloon, the seller must determine in good faith that the buyer can reasonably repay, and the same rate conditions apply.

Our worked example, with its five-year balloon, could fit the one-property exclusion but not the three-property exclusion.

**Creditor status.** Separately, Regulation Z treats someone who extends dwelling-secured credit more than five times in the preceding calendar year as a **creditor**, which triggers full disclosure and ability-to-repay obligations, per [12 CFR 1026.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1026/subpart-A/section-1026.2). High-cost loans have a lower threshold.

**SAFE Act licensing.** Every state licenses mortgage loan originators, and federal guidance has said that a person selling and financing their own residence is not likely to be "engaged in the business" of originating loans. But exemptions are set state by state, and a few states offer little or none, as [NAR's SAFE Act overview](https://www.nar.realtor/topics/seller-financing/the-safe-act) notes. A common solution is to have a licensed residential mortgage loan originator structure the loan.

## Due-on-sale risk when the seller still has a mortgage

Most mortgages include a **due-on-sale clause** that lets the lender demand full payment if the property is transferred. Federal law, the [Garn-St Germain Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title12/html/USCODE-2024-title12-chap13-sec1701j-3.htm), allows lenders to enforce these clauses, with narrow exceptions for homes of fewer than five units, such as transfers on death to a relative, to a spouse or children, in a divorce, or into a qualifying living trust.

Two points matter for owner financing:

1. A **wraparound** or "subject-to" sale, where the seller's loan stays in place while the buyer pays the seller, is a transfer. If the lender finds out, it can accelerate the loan.
2. The statute protects only leases of **three years or less that contain no option to purchase**. A lease-option falls outside that exception.

If the lender calls the loan and no one can pay it off, the property can go to foreclosure, and the buyer can lose everything paid in. Get the lender's written consent, or pay off the existing loan at closing.

## Balloon payments and refinancing risk

Balloons let sellers get their money back in a few years, but they shift risk to the buyer. If rates rise, the buyer's credit slips, or the home appraises below the balance, refinancing may be impossible. Protect both sides by negotiating a realistic balloon date, an option to extend for a fee, no prepayment penalty, and a requirement that the buyer start the refinance process at least six months before the due date.

## Pros and cons for buyers and sellers

| | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Buyer** | Flexible qualifying; faster closing; negotiable down payment and terms; fewer lender fees | Often a higher rate; balloon refinancing risk; due-on-sale risk if the seller has a loan; weaker protections under land contracts and lease-options |
| **Seller** | Larger buyer pool; possible higher price; interest income; installment-sale tax treatment may spread gain over years | Default and foreclosure risk; money tied up in the note; compliance obligations; servicing and record-keeping |

## Protect yourself: an owner-financing checklist

1. Hire a real estate attorney licensed in the property's state to draft the note, security instrument, and purchase contract.
2. Order a title search and owner's title insurance, plus a lender's policy for the seller.
3. Record the deed and the mortgage or deed of trust, or a memorandum of the land contract.
4. Use a third-party loan servicer to collect payments, send year-end interest statements, and keep records.
5. Escrow property taxes and insurance, and name the seller as mortgagee on the hazard policy.
6. Pull the buyer's credit and document income for an ability-to-repay file, even if not strictly required.
7. Check state usury limits, late-fee rules, and foreclosure procedures.
8. Have the seller meet a tax professional about installment-sale reporting before closing.
