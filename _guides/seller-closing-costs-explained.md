This guide is for homeowners, especially those selling by owner, who want to know exactly what will come out of the sale price before the money hits their account. After reading it, you'll be able to estimate each seller cost line by line, spot the ones that depend on your state, and negotiate the ones that are negotiable.

## What counts as a seller closing cost

Everything deducted from your sale price at closing falls into one of four buckets:

- **Loan payoffs:** your mortgage, any home equity line, and any other lien recorded against the property.
- **Third-party fees:** title, escrow or settlement, attorney, and any agent you hire.
- **Government charges:** transfer taxes and recording fees.
- **Prorations and credits:** your share of property taxes and HOA dues, plus anything you agreed to give the buyer.

In a sale with a mortgage lender, the settlement agent typically gives you a **seller's Closing Disclosure**, a version of the federal form that shows only your costs and credits. Cash sales often use a settlement statement instead. Either way, ask for a draft several days before closing and check every number against your contract.

## Line-item table: what sellers typically pay

The table below lists the costs you're likely to see. Amounts vary widely, so the right column explains how each one is set rather than quoting a national average.

| Cost | Paid to | How the amount is set |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage payoff | Your lender | Principal plus interest through the payoff date, plus any small release or fax fees on the payoff letter |
| Settlement or escrow fee | Title company or closing attorney | Flat fee per transaction; often split between buyer and seller by custom |
| Owner's title insurance | Title insurer | Based on the price; the seller pays in many areas and the buyer in others |
| Attorney fees | Your attorney | Flat fee or hourly; some states expect an attorney to handle closings |
| Transfer or deed tax | State, county, or city | A percentage of the price set by law, ranging from zero to several percent |
| Recording fees | County recorder | Small fees to record the release of your mortgage and sometimes the deed |
| Property tax proration | Credited to buyer, or to you | Day-count split based on whether local taxes are paid in advance or in arrears |
| HOA charges | Association or management company | Dues proration plus resale certificate, estoppel, or transfer fees, which some states cap |
| Seller concessions | Credited to buyer | Whatever you agree to cover, such as part of the buyer's closing costs |
| Agent commissions | Listing and/or buyer's agent | Fully negotiable; zero if you sell by owner to an unrepresented buyer |
| Repair credits | Credited to buyer | Negotiated after the inspection instead of doing the work yourself |
| Local point-of-sale items | City or inspector | Some cities require inspections or certificates before transfer |

Every line except the payoff is a cost you can influence through your contract, your choice of service providers, or your timing.

## Worked example: a $400,000 sale

Here's an illustrative by-owner sale. The seller has a mortgage, agreed to pay part of the buyer's closing costs, and agreed in the contract to pay the buyer's agent 2.5%. Closing is June 30, and local property taxes of $6,000 a year are paid in arrears.

- Sale price: **$400,000**
- Mortgage payoff, including interest to closing: $212,400
- Settlement fee (seller's half): $750
- Owner's title insurance (seller pays by local custom): $1,900
- Transfer tax at an assumed 1%: $4,000
- Attorney document review: $900
- Property tax proration credit to buyer: $2,959
- HOA resale certificate and dues proration: $350
- Recording the mortgage release: $100
- Seller concession toward buyer's costs: $5,000
- Buyer's agent fee at 2.5%: $10,000

**Total costs excluding the payoff: $25,959.** Net proceeds: $400,000 − $212,400 − $25,959 = **$161,641.**

The property tax line is worth unpacking. IRS Publication 530 treats the seller as paying property tax up to, but not including, the date of sale. From January 1 through June 29 is 180 days, so the seller's share is $6,000 × 180 ÷ 365 = $2,959. Because taxes here are paid in arrears, the buyer will pay the full bill later, so the seller credits that amount at closing. In an area where taxes are paid in advance, the credit runs the other way.

Strip out the buyer's agent fee and the concession, and this seller's costs drop to $10,959, under 3% of the price. That gap is why the two biggest negotiable items deserve the most attention. ## Costs that vary most by state and city

**Transfer taxes** are the biggest wild card. Pennsylvania, for example, charges a 1% state realty transfer tax, and most localities add their own, commonly another 1% and more in some places. The state holds buyer and seller jointly liable, even though contracts usually split the bill. Texas, by contrast, has no state transfer tax. Your county recorder's website will list current rates.

**Title insurance customs** decide whether you or the buyer pays for the owner's policy, and in some states the rates themselves are set by regulation.

**Attorney involvement** ranges from required to optional. In some states an attorney conducts the closing; in others a title or escrow company handles everything.

**Point-of-sale requirements** exist in some cities and counties: water or sewer inspections, smoke detector certificates, or code compliance letters. These can add both cost and time, so check before you sign a contract with a tight closing date.

Rules vary by state, so confirm local customs with a title company or real estate attorney before you set your price.

### Costs that surprise sellers

A few items don't show up in most online estimates but appear on real settlement statements:

- **Per diem interest:** mortgage interest is paid in arrears, so your payoff includes interest for the days since your last payment.
- **Special assessments:** an HOA or city assessment that's been levied but not fully paid may need to be paid off or credited at closing.
- **Energy and improvement liens:** financed solar panels or property-assessed clean energy (PACE) loans can be recorded against the property and usually must be paid off or formally assumed.
- **Final utility bills:** some areas hold back money at closing until a final water or sewer reading is in.

## How prorations and credits work

Prorations make sure each party pays for the time they owned the home. Beyond property taxes, expect prorations for HOA dues, prepaid special assessments, and, if the home is tenant-occupied, the current month's rent. Security deposits held for tenants usually transfer to the buyer as a credit.

Credits are different: they're money you agree to give the buyer at closing. A $5,000 closing-cost credit and a $5,000 price reduction cost you about the same, but they aren't identical. A credit can help a cash-strapped buyer close, while a price cut lowers the appraisal target and the buyer's loan amount. Lenders also limit how much a seller can contribute, so confirm the buyer's lender will allow any credit you offer.

## Ways to reduce your seller closing costs

1. **Negotiate the fee split in the contract.** Local custom on who pays title, escrow, or transfer tax is a starting point, not a requirement.
2. **Ask for a title reissue rate.** If you bought or refinanced in recent years, many title insurers offer a discount on a new owner's policy.
3. **Get settlement quotes.** Settlement and attorney fees vary between providers, so compare two or three before you list.
4. **Set a clear policy on buyer-agent compensation.** Decide in advance whether you'll pay a buyer's agent and how much. Our guide to [real estate commission rule changes](/guides/real-estate-commission-rule-changes-explained/) explains your options.
5. **Fix small issues before listing.** A $300 repair you handle now beats a $2,000 credit negotiated under pressure after the inspection.
6. **Order your payoff letter early.** Request it a week or more before closing so the title company can confirm the figure and avoid delays that add interest days.
7. **Verify wiring instructions by phone.** Call your title company or attorney at a number you already know before you rely on any emailed instructions for your proceeds or payoff.

## The tax angle

Most seller costs, including commissions, title charges, legal fees, and advertising, are **selling expenses** under IRS Publication 523. They don't show up as deductions; instead they reduce your amount realized, which lowers your gain. Your share of property taxes up to the sale date may be deductible if you itemize. Keep the final signed closing statement with your tax records; you'll need it if you receive a Form 1099-S.
