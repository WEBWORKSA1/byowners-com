This guide is for homeowners who want to sell without a listing agent and keep more of their equity. By the end, you'll know every step from the decision to the closing table, what each step costs, and where a professional is worth paying by the hour instead of by the percentage.

## The FSBO roadmap at a glance

Selling by owner is a sequence of about a dozen jobs. A listing agent bundles them into one fee; you'll unbundle them, do most yourself, and hire out the few that carry legal risk.

1. **Decide** whether FSBO fits your house, your market, and your schedule.
2. **Prepare** the property: repairs, cleaning, and photos.
3. **Price** it from recent closed sales.
4. **Gather documents**: deed, mortgage payoff, disclosures, HOA paperwork.
5. **List** it on by-owner sites and, if you want MLS reach, through a flat-fee broker.
6. **Market** it with a strong description, a yard sign, and open houses.
7. **Show** it by appointment to screened buyers.
8. **Collect offers** in writing.
9. **Negotiate** price, terms, and dates.
10. **Sign a contract** drafted or reviewed by a real estate attorney.
11. **Manage the inspection and appraisal** contingencies.
12. **Close** through a title company, escrow agent, or attorney.

## Step 1: Decide whether selling by owner fits

FSBO is a minority path. NAR's 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers, which surveyed people who sold between mid-2024 and mid-2025, found that only 5% sold without an agent, the lowest share on record. That doesn't mean it can't work. It means you need a reason, a plan, and realistic expectations.

Selling by owner tends to go smoothly when your neighborhood has steady sales (so pricing is straightforward), your house is conventional, you have time for evening and weekend showings, and you can talk about money without flinching. It gets harder with unusual property, a hard deadline, a probate or divorce sale, or a slow market where buyers have leverage.

### FSBO vs. traditional agent vs. flat-fee MLS

| Factor | FSBO (no broker) | Traditional listing agent | Flat-fee MLS listing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listing-side cost | No commission; you pay for photos, sign, and attorney | Percentage commission, negotiable | One upfront fee, commonly a few hundred dollars |
| Buyer's agent pay | Your choice, negotiated offer by offer | Set with your agent, communicated off-MLS | Your choice, negotiated offer by offer |
| Control | Total: price, showings, negotiation | Agent advises; you approve | Total; broker mainly enters the listing |
| Market exposure | By-owner sites, sign, social, word of mouth | MLS, public-site syndication, agent network | MLS and public-site syndication |
| Your effort | Highest | Lowest | High |
| Best for | Hot markets, simple homes, a buyer already lined up | Complex sales, sellers short on time | Sellers who want MLS reach and will do the rest |

Here's the math in dollars. On a $400,000 sale, an illustrative 5.5% total commission is $22,000. If you sell by owner and agree to pay a buyer's agent 2.5%, you pay $10,000 and keep the other $12,000, minus your marketing and legal costs. If the buyer is unrepresented, you keep nearly all of it. These figures are examples; commissions are negotiable and vary by market. Run your own numbers in the [commission savings calculator](/tools/commission-savings-calculator/).

## Step 2: Prepare the house

Buyers decide whether to visit based on photos, so preparation is marketing. Spend your first two weeks here.

- **Fix the small stuff.** Dripping faucets, sticky doors, missing outlet covers, and dead bulbs end up as line items in the buyer's inspection report, and a long list makes a sound house look neglected.
- **Consider a pre-listing inspection.** For a few hundred dollars you learn what the buyer's inspector will find, on your schedule rather than mid-negotiation. Anything it reveals may need to go on your disclosure form, so read it before you order one.
- **Declutter by a third.** Clear counters, thin closets, and remove personal photos. Rent a storage unit if you need one; it's cheaper than a price cut.
- **Photograph in daylight.** Shoot every room horizontally from a corner at chest height, plus the front, back, and street. Hire a real estate photographer if your phone shots look dark. Listing photos are your highest-return expense.

## Step 3: Price it from closed sales

Overpricing is the most expensive FSBO mistake, because buyers and their agents already expect owners to be optimistic. Price from what buyers actually paid, not from what neighbors are asking.

Pull three to six closed sales from the past six months, close by and similar in size, age, and bed/bath count. Then do the price-per-square-foot math. Example: comps sold at $412,000 for 2,000 square feet ($206), $395,000 for 1,900 ($208), and $428,000 for 2,100 ($204). The average is about $206, so a 1,950-square-foot house points to roughly $401,700 before adjusting for a renovated kitchen, an extra bath, or a bigger lot.

Then think like a buyer searching online. A house listed at $399,900 shows up in searches capped at $400,000; one listed at $405,000 doesn't. Our full method, including adjustments and when to cut, is in [how to price a home for sale by owner](/guides/how-to-price-a-home-for-sale-by-owner/).

## Step 4: Gather documents and disclosures

Assemble your paperwork before the sign goes up, so an eager buyer doesn't cool off while you hunt for a deed.

- **Deed and title.** Confirm whose names are on title. Ask a title company for a preliminary report to surface old liens, judgments, or an unreleased mortgage while there's time to fix them.
- **Mortgage payoff.** Request a payoff statement from your servicer so you know your real equity.
- **State disclosure form.** Many states require a written property condition disclosure. Answer every question honestly and attach repair records.
- **Lead-based paint (pre-1978 homes).** Federal law requires you to give the buyer the EPA pamphlet, disclose any known lead-based paint or hazards and related reports, include the lead warning language in the contract, and offer a 10-day window for a lead inspection unless the buyer waives it in writing. Keep the signed forms for at least three years. See the [EPA's real estate disclosure page](https://www.epa.gov/lead/protect-your-family-lead-your-home-real-estate-disclosure).
- **HOA documents.** If you have an association, order the resale packet early; some take weeks.
- **Purchase agreement.** Use a state-specific form, ideally prepared or reviewed by a real estate attorney.

Rules vary by state, so confirm the requirements with a local real estate attorney.

## Step 5: List and market the home

Put the house on by-owner listing sites, and decide whether a flat-fee MLS listing is worth it to reach agents' buyers. Write a description that leads with the three things that make the home different, uses numbers (roof year, square footage, commute), and ends with clear showing instructions.

Add a yard sign with a short web address or QR code, and hold an open house the first weekend, when interest peaks. Describe the property, not the people you hope will buy it. The Fair Housing Act's advertising rules apply even to owners selling their own homes, so "three bedrooms near the elementary school" is fine and "perfect for a young family" is not.

### Decide your buyer's-agent policy now

Since August 17, 2024, offers of compensation to buyers' agents can't be published in MLS listings, and agents must sign written agreements with buyers before touring homes. Many buyers will arrive with an agent and ask you to cover some or all of that agent's fee. Decide in advance whether you'll pay a fee, offer a closing-cost credit, or neither. Whatever you agree to belongs in the purchase contract.

## Step 6: Show the house safely

Show by appointment. Get each visitor's full name, check a photo ID at the door, and ask for a lender pre-approval letter before a private showing. Lock away valuables, prescriptions, and mail. Have a second adult at home, tell a friend the schedule, and walk behind visitors rather than ahead. At open houses, use a sign-in sheet and keep bedroom doors open.

During the visit, say less than you want to. Point out the new water heater, answer questions honestly, and let buyers imagine living there.

## Step 7: Evaluate offers and negotiate

Insist that every offer be in writing on a purchase agreement. Then compare the whole package: price, financing type, down payment, earnest money, contingencies, closing date, and any credits or fees the buyer wants you to pay.

The highest price isn't always the best offer. A $410,000 offer that asks for $8,000 in credits and depends on the buyer selling another home nets less and carries more risk than a $402,000 conventional offer with 20% down and a short inspection period.

Counter in writing with a firm deadline, usually 24 to 48 hours. With multiple offers, tell every buyer and ask for best and final terms by a set time. Before you sign, have your attorney review the contract, especially deadlines and what happens to the earnest money if the deal fails.

## Step 8: Get through inspection and appraisal

Most contracts give the buyer a set number of days for inspections. Expect a repair request. You can make repairs, offer a credit, lower the price, or decline. Credits are often cleanest, because you aren't managing contractors on the buyer's timeline.

If the buyer is financing, the lender orders an appraisal. If it comes in below the contract price, the usual options are to lower the price, have the buyer cover the gap in cash, split the difference, or cancel under the appraisal contingency. Pricing from closed comps in Step 3 is your best defense.

Between contract and closing, keep the house in the condition it was in when the buyer signed: utilities on, lawn mowed, and every fixture that was included left in place. The buyer usually gets a final walk-through shortly before closing to confirm agreed repairs are finished, so keep receipts from the contractors who did them.

## Step 9: Close the sale

Depending on your state and local custom, a title company, escrow agent, or real estate attorney handles the closing. They search title, order your payoff, prepare the settlement statement and deed, collect the buyer's funds, pay off your loan, disburse your proceeds, and record the deed.

Wire fraud is the biggest closing-day risk. The CFPB advises verifying wiring instructions by calling a phone number you already know, never one from an email, and treating any last-minute change as a red flag. Give your own payout instructions in person or by a verified call.

On taxes, the IRS lets you exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for most married couples filing jointly, if you owned and lived in the home for at least two of the five years before the sale. Details and exceptions are in [IRS Publication 523](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p523).

## Mistakes that sink FSBO sales

- Pricing from active listings or a neighbor's anecdote instead of closed sales.
- Skimping on photos and then blaming the market.
- Accepting a verbal offer or a contract with blank deadlines.
- Forgetting the lead-based paint disclosure on a pre-1978 home.
- Letting an emailed wiring instruction go unverified.
